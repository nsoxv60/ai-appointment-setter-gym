# ai appointment setter for gyms: real monthly costs, 24/7 booking, and the no-show leak most clubs never fix

Someone taps your Instagram ad at 9:40pm on a Sunday, fills in the free trial form, and waits. Your front desk opens at 6am. By then they've messaged two other clubs in the same suburb, and one of them replied while they were still holding their phone.

That gap — not the ad creative, not the offer — is where most gyms quietly lose trials they already paid for. An AI appointment setter is one way to close it. Whether it's worth it depends on which channels you're losing leads in, what you're currently paying a human to do in those channels, and how much of the conversation you actually want a bot owning.

Here's what these tools really do for a gym, what they cost right now, and where they fall short.

## What the job actually is

Strip away the branding and an AI setter does five things in a text thread:

1. Reply in seconds, at any hour, in whatever channel the lead used
2. Ask qualifying questions — goal, timeline, location, budget, commitment level
3. Book a trial, tour, or intro session onto a specific calendar slot
4. Handle reschedules and cancellations without a human touching it
5. Keep following up until the person shows, books again, or clearly isn't interested

CloseBot is one of the better-known tools in this space, and it's built specifically for that job — qualifying leads and booking appointments across the text channels already sitting inside your CRM. It connects natively to HighLevel, HubSpot, and custom CRM setups, and it can also run standalone without a CRM at all.

The thing worth flagging early, because most "AI receptionist for gyms" articles blur it: **CloseBot works in text channels, not voice.** That means SMS, email, Instagram DMs, Facebook Messenger, and website chat. It doesn't pick up the phone. If your missed calls are the bigger problem, you need a voice product — or you route calls into a text follow-up, which is what a lot of clubs do anyway.

## Two leaks, and only one of them is about speed

### 1. Speed to lead

The commonly cited benchmark here is the five-minute rule: leads contacted within five minutes are far more likely to qualify than leads contacted after 30 minutes. ABC Glofox's writeup on gym lead response puts that multiple at 21x, and notes the average business takes over 40 hours to respond to an inbound inquiry. Glofox also points out that only about 2% of sales close on first contact, and that clubs with structured follow-up systems convert somewhere in the 15–20% range of qualified leads.

Read that again with a gym owner's brain. The inquiry you're slowest on is the one that arrives at 8pm, when your team is on the floor coaching. It clusters around evenings, weekends, and January.

### 2. No-shows after the booking

Booking a trial isn't revenue. A study from Keepme — 248 trial and tour requests submitted as a mystery shopper across 75 multi-site operators covering roughly 6,500 locations, run between March and April 2025 — found that **1 in 7 gyms made no follow-up attempt at all after a no-show**, and **55% failed to send even a single pre-visit reminder**. The prospect confirmed a time slot and then heard nothing.

That's the leak an AI setter is genuinely good at plugging, because persistent, low-cost, multi-touch follow-up is exactly what humans are bad at and software is fine at.

CloseBot's own published benchmark adds context. Across more than 1.1 million appointments booked by its agents, just over half happened outside 9am–5pm in the lead's local time, roughly 11% landed between midnight and 6am, and the company recorded one lead who booked **355 days** after first contact, through a follow-up sequence that never expired. Most default follow-up windows agencies configure run 30 days and give up long before that tail converts.

Their data also suggests Sunday is the cheapest day of the week to convert and book a slot — Sunday leads close with fewer messages than any other day, while Friday bookings take about 29% more messages than Sunday ones. A club that lets Sunday enquiries sit in an inbox until Monday morning is ignoring its most motivated traffic.

## Where CloseBot fits into a gym's stack

The build interface is a drag-and-drop flow builder, not a prompt box. You define objectives (collect the phone number, confirm location, book the right class type), add knowledge, set the tools the agent can call, and test the whole conversation in a testing portal before it goes live.

Practical features that matter for a fitness business:

- **Calendar-aware booking.** The agent books onto a named calendar set by a CRM workflow, so it can route "intro session" and "group class" leads to different calendars.
- **Conversational rescheduling and cancellation**, including across different appointment types, from the same agent.
- **Human takeover.** You can pause the AI on any single conversation and step in.
- **Smart FAQ.** When the agent can't answer something, it flags the question; once you answer it, CloseBot re-engages everyone who asked.
- **Channel coverage.** Messenger, Instagram, SMS, email, live chat. In the Raise-the-bar note: it can read images sent by leads.
- **Language support.** The company says 40+ languages and counting.

The most concrete gym numbers come from a vendor-published case study on Flex Appeal, a gym in a border town targeting Spanish-speaking members, working with agency Traction Lab. Before: lead response time of more than four days, 36% of leads providing contact info, roughly 70% churn, and two failed attempts to open a second location. After implementing a CloseBot agent named "Vicky" in July 2024, tuned to northern Mexican Spanish, the reported results were:

- Response time dropped from 4+ days to **33 seconds**
- Lead conversion rose from 36% to **53%** in the first month
- Qualified leads increased 17% (331 vs 282) despite 18% fewer inbound leads
- Churn fell from about 70% to roughly **20%**
- Annual revenue went from $655,900 (2023) to $805,300 (2024)
- A February ad spend of $3,000 produced $25,000 in sales

Treat those as one agency's account of one client, not a benchmark. The transferable part is the build philosophy the agency described: two or three objectives, not ten. Interest, location, phone number. Their AI also had its reply delay deliberately slowed from 9 seconds to 33 seconds to feel less robotic — a small detail that says more about gym buyers than most feature lists do.

## Gym owner direct, or through an agency?

Two buying paths, and the price gap between them is large.

**Direct (Business plan):** you or someone on your team builds the agent. Entry cost is $64/month with 500 messages included, and most teams are told they can get a first agent live the same day. This is the right path if you have one or two locations, a straightforward offer, and someone who enjoys systems work.

**Through an agency (Agency plan resold to you):** the agency builds, manages, and bills you. CloseBot's own figures — based on a poll from its agency users — put the average agency billing at about $500 per client per month. In the Flex Appeal case study, Traction Lab charged the gym $500/month for the implementation and management.

The direct-to-CloseBot option is much cheaper on paper. The honest trade-off is that the build quality is what determines results, not the subscription. A badly configured agent that asks nine qualifying questions before offering a time slot will convert worse than a human who answers in 90 minutes.

## What the build actually looks like

For a single gym, most of the work is done once and then tuned:

1. **Pick two or three objectives.** Interest level, location, phone number. The Flex Appeal build used exactly this. Adding objectives multiplies failure modes.
2. **Connect the CRM and calendar.** HighLevel and HubSpot are native; custom CRM setups connect too.
3. **Write the qualifying questions in the gym's actual voice.** Front desk language, not corporate.
4. **Test in the testing portal.**

5. **Set the response delay deliberately.** Instant is faster; 30 seconds often reads as human.
6. **Decide the handoff rules.** Membership pricing negotiation, complaints, and injury questions are usually worth a human.
7. **Go live in one channel first.** SMS or Instagram, whichever your leads actually use.
8. **Watch the Smart FAQ flags in week one.** They tell you what your website doesn't answer.

One agency-side review of CloseBot for HighLevel users puts initial setup at 5–10 hours for a solid build. CloseBot's own marketing says most teams get live the same day. Both can be true — a simple flow is quick, a good one takes an afternoon.

## Six things to check before you pay

- **Voice is not included.** If a third of your enquiries are phone calls, a text setter is a partial fix.
- **Your AI provider token costs are separate on usage-based plans**, and CloseBot's documentation notes that V2 requires your own API keys. Model that cost before quoting anyone a price.
- **Overage is real.** Business plans charge 2x per message over the ceiling, pulled from your wallet. A lead who keeps replying at 1am is not free.
- **The 1.1 million appointment dataset is CloseBot's own**, skewed toward SMS-first workflows. It's the largest public sample in this category, but it's the vendor's data about the vendor's users.
- **No refunds.** Use the 7-day trial properly — build the real flow, not a demo.
- **Third-party reviews are broadly positive but thin on gyms specifically.** G2 lists CloseBot at 4.8 from around 190 verified reviews, with users praising setup speed and conversational booking. Most of those reviewers are agencies and B2B sellers, not fitness operators.

## So does a gym need one?

If your leads arrive by text somewhere — Instagram DMs, Facebook, SMS, a website chat widget — and nobody is answering between 6pm and 6am, an AI setter is one of the few things that fixes the problem without hiring a night shift.

For a single-site club with 30–50 leads a month, a $64/month Business plan plus message overage is a modest bet against a single lost annual membership. For a multi-location operator, the interesting version isn't the setter at all — it's the follow-up tail that never quits: the lead who doesn't book in October and signs up in July, and the no-show who gets a reminder regardless of whether the front desk remembered.

That's the part humans lose. The rest — pricing conversations, retention saves, anything emotional — should still go to a person, and the decent tools let you hand it over mid-thread.
